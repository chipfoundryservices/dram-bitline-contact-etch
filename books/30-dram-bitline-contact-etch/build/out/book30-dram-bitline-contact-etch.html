Preface

Every cell pair in a buried-channel DRAM shares one connection to its bit line. That connection is the bit-line contact, also called the direct contact (DC): a hole about 30 nm across, etched through a thin insulating buffer, landing on the centre of the active area between two buried word lines, and recessed a few tens of nanometres into the silicon before it is filled with doped polysilicon. When the bit-line stack is etched later (Book 29), the line passes over this plug and the plug becomes the bit line's foot in the cell.

The contact is small and shallow by the standards of other contact etches, but its floor is unusual. It does not land on one material. At the bottom of a single hole there is the silicon of the bit-line node, the oxide of the shallow trench isolation on either side of it, the silicon-nitride caps of the two word lines that bracket it, and between silicon and cap the edge of each word line's gate oxide, a sliver only 3.5 nm wide. The DC etch recesses all of these at once, and every recipe makes a different shape out of them. A silicon-selective recipe digs a pit in the node. A non-selective recipe moves the whole floor down together. An oxide-selective recipe leaves the node standing as a mesa and can drill down the gate-oxide liner toward the word line.

Four outcomes are decided at this step. The contact area on the node, together with the interface left by the etch and clean, sets the contact resistance in series with every read and write. The cap and liner loss sets the margin between the bit-line plug and the word line beneath it. The clearance from the hole to the neighbouring storage-node silicon decides whether the plug touches a cell it should not. The damage and residue left in the silicon set leakage and resistance drift. A few nanometres of overlay or a few seconds of recipe decide all four.

This book treats the bit-line contact etch as a problem of transport, selectivity and geometry. It has four parts and sixteen chapters, with seven appendices, a glossary and an index.

Part I — Foundations (Chapters 1–4): the contact's role in the cell, the stack and process flow, the chemistry of a mixed floor, and a computed treatment of ion and neutral transport into a round hole.

Part II — Contact Etch Physics (Chapters 5–8): the mask open and CD shrink, the buffer open, the computed recess of the mixed floor, and the attack on the word-line cap and gate-oxide liner.

Part III — Contact Formation and Integration (Chapters 9–12): profile and bottom CD, the computed contact area and resistance, damage and the pre-fill clean, and the polysilicon fill and hand-off to the bit-line stack.

Part IV — Control, Variation and Outlook (Chapters 13–16): reactor, recipe, endpoint and metrology; the computed variation budget; yield and reliability; and the contact in 4F² and 3D DRAM.

Ion and neutral transport into a round hole (Chapter 4, Appendix B), the buffer clear times (Chapter 6), the recess histories of the four floor materials under three recipe families (Chapters 7 and 8), the contact area, clearance and resistance against overlay (Chapter 10) and the variation budget (Chapter 14) are computed by sim/contact_mc.py. Part I — Foundations

Chapter 1 — The Bit-Line Contact in the Cell

1.1 One contact for two cells

A DRAM cell stores a bit as charge on a capacitor and reads it through an access transistor onto a bit line. In the buried-channel array transistor (BCAT) used in modern 6F² DRAM, the transistor gate is a buried word line, and each active area (AA) is a short silicon bar crossed by two word lines. The bar therefore holds three diffusions. The two ends are storage nodes, each connected upward through a storage-node contact (SNC, also called a buried contact) to its own capacitor. The centre, between the two word lines, is the bit-line node, shared by the two cells. The bit-line contact connects that centre node to the bit line.

Because the node is shared, there is one bit-line contact for every two cells. In a 16 Gb die that is 8 × 10⁹ contacts, each of which must conduct, and each of which must stay isolated from the storage nodes and word lines packed around it.

1.2 What the contact must do

The bit-line contact has four jobs:

  1. Conduct. The read signal is a small charge shared between the cell capacitor and the bit line. The write must drive the cell to full level within a few nanoseconds. Contact resistance is in series with both. In the reference process it is about 6 kΩ, a sizeable fraction of the access path resistance. At the specification edge, slow writes appear as tWR failures and weak reads appear as sense-margin loss.
  2. Insulate from the word lines. The contact lands between two word lines whose metal tops lie 60 nm below the silicon surface under a nitride cap. Any recess of the cap or of the gate-oxide liner beneath the contact brings the n⁺ plug toward the word-line metal. That raises the field across the cap, adds coupling between bit line and word line, and in the limit causes a short.
  3. Insulate from the neighbouring storage nodes. In the band between two word lines, the bit-line node of one active area sits beside storage-node ends of adjacent active areas, separated by STI. A hole that strays onto them connects a bit line to the wrong cell.
  4. Leave the junction healthy. The etch exposes the node silicon to ions and halogens. Damage, residues and interfacial oxide raise contact resistance and add leakage to the bit-line junction.

1.3 Dimensions

With F = 17 nm, the 6F² cell occupies 34 nm × 51 nm = 1734 nm². The word-line pitch is 34 nm and each word-line trench is 18 nm wide, so the silicon between two word lines is 16 nm long along the bit line. The active area is 16 nm wide and tilted 21° from the bit line, so the node is a parallelogram 16 nm long and 16/cos 21° = 17.1 nm tall, with an area of 274 nm². This is the most silicon any contact can touch on the node's top surface.

The contact hole is larger than the node. In the reference process it is 32 nm across at the top of the buffer and 26 nm at the silicon surface. The hole therefore overhangs the node on all sides. In x (along the bit line) it overhangs onto the word-line caps. In y it overhangs onto STI oxide. The larger hole is deliberate. It makes the contact area insensitive to small CD and overlay errors, at the price of exposing other materials in the floor (Chapter 10).

1.4 Where the contact sits in the flow

The DC module follows the word-line module (trench, gate oxide, metal recess, cap fill and planarisation; Books 26–28) and precedes the bit-line stack (Book 29):

  1. planarised surface with word-line caps flush with silicon and STI;
  2. buffer deposition (oxide and nitride);
  3. DC lithography on a carbon hard mask, with CD shrink;
  4. DC etch: mask open, buffer open, mixed-floor recess;
  5. strip, clean and in-situ doped polysilicon fill;
  6. poly planarisation or etchback to the buffer top;
  7. bit-line stack deposition and etch, which also trims the DC poly beside the line.

1.6 Why this etch is hard

The DC etch is short (about a minute of plasma). It is not deep, with an aspect ratio below 5. It is hard for three other reasons. Its floor is four materials, so the floor's shape after etching depends on selectivities the engineer chooses and on transport into sub-features only a few nanometres wide. Its margins are set by overlay to a tilted active area, so the same error costs different amounts in different directions. Its failure modes (high resistance, word-line coupling, neighbour shorts, leakage) appear only at electrical test, weeks later. The rest of this book develops each of these points.

Chapter 2 — Stack, Layout and Process Flow

2.1 The incoming surface

The DC etch starts on a surface prepared by the word-line module. After the word-line metal is recessed (Book 28) and the cap nitride is deposited and planarised, the surface is a mosaic. It holds silicon active areas, STI oxide between them, and nitride-capped word-line trenches crossing both. The caps are flush with the silicon to within a few nanometres. Any step left by cap CMP is inherited by the DC floor.

Under the cap, each word-line trench is lined with gate oxide, 3.5 nm in the reference process. At the surface this liner appears as a thin oxide line between the silicon and the cap on both sides of every word line. Its top edge is exposed in the DC floor wherever the hole overhangs a word line, which is everywhere.

2.2 The buffer

A thin buffer is deposited before the DC mask. The reference buffer is 6 nm of SiO₂ under 14 nm of SiN, 20 nm in all. The buffer has three roles. It insulates the bit line from the silicon and word-line caps everywhere except at the DC. It acts as the etch stop for the bit-line stack etch (Book 29). It also protects the active-area surface from the DC mask processes.

Opening the buffer is the first part of the DC etch (Chapter 6). The buffer's oxide layer sits directly on the silicon and STI. The nitride layer is the stop that the bit-line etch later lands on, so the DC etch must not thin it outside the hole.

2.3 The mask

DC holes at this pitch are printed by immersion lithography, sometimes with double patterning or EUV. They are transferred into a carbon hard mask, either amorphous carbon (ACL) or spin-on carbon, under a SiON or SiARC cap. The lithographic hole is larger than the final contact, about 42 nm in the reference process. It is shrunk to 32 nm by depositing polymer on the hole walls during the SiON and carbon open (Chapter 5). The carbon left at the start of the recess step is about 70 nm, which dominates the aspect ratio seen by the floor.

2.4 The layout seen from above

The layout fixes the geometry the etch has to work with. Take x along the bit line and y along the word line. Word-line trenches occupy 8 < |x| < 26 nm on either side of the contact. The node silicon is the part of the tilted active-area strip with |x| < 8 nm. Neighbouring strips, which carry storage-node ends of adjacent cells, repeat every 32/cos 21° = 34.3 nm in y. A 26 nm hole centred on the node covers:

  • the whole node, about 272 of its 274 nm² (the hole is node-limited);
  • about 143 nm² of word-line cap nitride, as two lunes at |x| > 8 nm;
  • about 116 nm² of STI oxide above and below the node;
  • the two gate-oxide liner slivers where the hole crosses the trench walls.

Its edge is 11 nm from the nearest neighbouring strip.

2.6 The quantities that matter downstream

The bit-line stack etch (Book 29) inherits two things from the DC module: the height of the poly plug relative to the buffer, and the width of the DC relative to the bit line. The width sets the gaps beside the line that the bit-line etch must clear of poly (Book 29, Chapter 8). The storage-node contact etch inherits the cap and STI recess left beside the DC. A deep recess there becomes a deeper pocket that the spacer and SNC must deal with.

Chapter 3 — Etch Chemistry of a Mixed Floor

3.1 Three materials, three chemistries

A fluorocarbon plasma etches oxide and nitride well. A bromine–chlorine plasma etches silicon well. No single chemistry etches silicon, oxide and nitride at the same rate across the range of ion energy and polymer coverage met in production. The DC recess step chooses which material leads.

3.2 Fluorocarbon etching of oxide and nitride

In CF₄, CHF₃, C₄F₆ or C₄F₈ plasmas, a fluorocarbon film forms on all surfaces. Ions mix it into the substrate and drive the reactions. On SiO₂, the oxygen released from the film consumes carbon, so the film stays thin and etching proceeds. On silicon, no oxygen is released, the film thickens and etching slows. This is the basis of oxide-to-silicon selectivity. On SiN, nitrogen leaves as CN and the film is intermediate, so nitride etch rates lie between oxide and silicon. Hydrogen (CHF₃, CH₂F₂) increases nitride etching relative to oxide. Lean gases (CF₄, O₂ added) reduce selectivity overall.

Three regimes are useful at the DC floor:

  • Lean (CF₄/CHF₃/Ar, high ion energy): oxide, nitride and silicon at comparable rates. This is the co-recess family, labelled B in this book.
  • Polymerising (C₄F₆/O₂/Ar): oxide fast, nitride moderate, silicon very slow. This is the dielectric-first family, C.
  • Hydrofluorocarbon (CH₂F₂/O₂, CH₃F/O₂): nitride faster than oxide. It is used for the SiN part of the buffer.

3.3 Halogen etching of silicon

In HBr/Cl₂/O₂, silicon etches as SiBrₓ and SiClₓ. The products are volatile at room temperature under ion bombardment. Oxide and nitride etch slowly because the Si–O and Si–N bonds are stronger and oxygen passivates the surface. Selectivities of 30:1 to silicon over oxide are routine at low bias. This is the silicon-selective family, A. Oxygen added to the plasma forms a thin SiOₓBrᵧ layer on the sidewalls that keeps the pit anisotropic. Too much oxygen grows an oxide on the floor that stops etching.

3.5 Selectivity in a pit is not selectivity in the open

Selectivity measured on blanket films is a ratio of open-field rates. At the DC floor, each material sits at a different depth and in a patch of different width. The silicon node is 16 nm wide, STI patches about 8 nm, cap lunes about 5 nm, and the gate-oxide liner 3.5 nm. A material that etches faster forms a pit. Transport into that pit falls as it deepens (Chapter 4), so its rate drops. The effective selectivity at the end of the step is therefore lower than the blanket value, and lowest for the narrowest patches. Chapter 7 computes this.

3.6 Residues

Each family leaves its own residue. HBr/Cl₂/O₂ leaves bromine and chlorine in a thin oxidised layer on the silicon. Fluorocarbon chemistries leave a carbon–fluorine film on the floor and fluorine in the top nanometres of silicon. Both raise contact resistance if not removed. Chapter 11 discusses the strip and clean that remove them.

3.7 Temperature

Silicon etch in HBr/Cl₂ is weakly temperature dependent. Fluorocarbon polymer deposition is strongly temperature dependent: colder surfaces collect more polymer, raising selectivity and slowing oxide. A radial temperature profile across the chuck therefore becomes a radial profile of floor shape. Recipe C is the most sensitive, recipe A the least.

Chapter 4 — Transport into a Contact Hole

4.1 The geometry

At the start of the recess, the floor of the DC lies under 70 nm of carbon mask and 20 nm of buffer. For a 26 nm hole the aspect ratio, measured from the top of the mask, is 90/26 = 3.5. By the end of a 25 nm recess it is about 4.4 for a flat floor. Gas pressures are a few to tens of millitorr, so the mean free path (millimetres) is far larger than the hole. Transport inside it is in the Knudsen regime, and particles collide only with walls.

4.2 Neutrals

A neutral enters the hole with a cosine angular distribution. At each wall hit it is lost (reacts or recombines) with probability s. Otherwise it is re-emitted diffusely. The floor fraction is the fraction that reaches the floor. For s = 1 it equals the view factor from the opening to the floor, which for a cylinder of depth H and radius r is

F = ½ (X − √(X² − 4)), X = 2 + 4 AR², AR = H / 2r.

The Monte Carlo reproduces this limit within counting error at every aspect ratio (Appendix A).

4.3 Ions

Ions arrive nearly perpendicular to the wafer. Their angular spread σ is set by the sheath and the ion temperature, about 2° at the higher bias of recipes B and C and about 4° at the low bias of recipe A. In the model an ion travels in a straight line and is lost on any wall hit. Because the hole is convex, an ion reaches the floor exactly when its landing point lies inside the floor disk. Specular reflection and charging, which can deflect ions, are neglected.

4.5 Consequences for the DC

  • The mask dominates the aspect ratio. 70 of the 90 nm above the floor is carbon. Thinning the mask at the start of the recess (or stripping it and using the buffer as the mask) moves the floor from AR ≈ 3.5 to AR ≈ 0.8. This changes the neutral-to-ion balance at the floor completely.
  • Neutral-driven etching slows most. Recipe A depends more on neutrals than B or C. At AR 3.5 with s = 0.1 its neutral term is only 11 % of its open-field value, while the ion term is still about 62 %.
  • Sub-features see their own aspect ratio. A silicon pit 16 nm wide and 25 nm deep has AR 1.6 of its own. A pit in the 3.5 nm gate-oxide liner reaches AR 5 after only 17 nm. Chapter 7 multiplies the hole and pit transport fractions to estimate each material's rate.

4.6 Limits of the model

The model treats a straight-walled cylinder. A tapered hole delivers slightly more flux to the floor. It also takes s as constant and independent of surface coverage, ignores flux re-emitted from the floor and etch products, and ignores charging. Charging matters more here than in the bit-line etch because the hole is lined by insulators down to the floor. Its effect, a deflection of low-energy ions toward the walls, would further reduce the ion fraction at small σ. Appendix B tabulates all transport values.

Part II — Contact Etch Physics

Chapter 5 — Mask Open and CD Shrink

5.1 Why shrink

Lithography prints contact holes most reliably at a diameter well above the one the cell wants. A 42 nm printed hole has better dose latitude, depth of focus and local CD uniformity (LCDU) than a 32 nm one. The extra 10 nm is removed by the etch, which narrows the hole as it transfers it into the carbon hard mask. This CD shrink is the first thing the DC etch has to do, and it is the main control on the final contact size.

5.2 How the shrink is made

Two mechanisms are used, often together:

  • Polymer deposition on the walls. During the SiON open, a polymerising fluorocarbon (CHF₃, CH₂F₂) coats the walls of the resist and SiON hole. During the carbon open, gases such as CH₄, COS or a small CHF₃ addition to an O₂ or N₂/H₂ plasma deposit carbon or sulphur-containing passivation on the carbon walls faster than ions can remove it. The hole narrows by the passivation thickness on each side.
  • Taper. If the walls are passivated faster than the floor is etched sideways, the carbon wall slopes inward. A carbon layer 110 nm thick with a wall angle of 87.4° narrows a hole by 2 × 110 × cot 87.4° ≈ 10 nm.

In the reference process the hole leaves the carbon at 32 nm. The buffer etch then tapers it further to 26 nm at the silicon surface (Chapter 9).

5.3 Shrink and local CD uniformity

A shrink of fixed thickness, applied to every hole, reduces the mean CD but not its spread. If the deposition fills small holes relatively faster than large ones, as it does when the passivation flux depends on the hole's view of the plasma, the shrink also compresses the spread. A hole that prints small sees less passivant per unit wall area and narrows less. In practice, the hole-to-hole 3σ falls from about 2.5 nm after lithography to about 1.5–2 nm after the carbon open. The CD scatter used in Chapter 14 (σ = 1 nm at the buffer top) assumes this.

The shrink cannot compress errors that come from the mask itself or from placement. Overlay is untouched by shrink. A smaller hole tolerates less of it (Chapter 10).

5.4 The carbon budget

The carbon mask must survive the buffer open and the recess. Fluorocarbon chemistries (recipes B and C, and the buffer open) erode carbon at perhaps a fifth to a tenth of the oxide rate. HBr/Cl₂/O₂ (recipe A) erodes it slowly, because the oxygen content is small. A 110 nm carbon layer typically arrives at the recess step with 60–80 nm left; the reference value is 70 nm. Facets at the top corners of the carbon widen the top of the hole as it erodes, but at these thicknesses faceting rarely reaches the buffer.

The mask height matters to the floor more than to the mask. As Chapter 4 showed, 70 of the 90 nm the floor sits below the mask top is carbon. Every 10 nm of carbon removed lowers the aspect ratio by about 0.4 and raises the neutral flux to the floor by roughly 20 % at s = 0.1. Mask erosion during a long recess therefore speeds it up slowly. This drift is ignored in the model, which holds the mask at 70 nm.

5.5 Striations and hole shape

Resist line-edge roughness becomes hole-edge roughness. Polymerising shrinks smooth it somewhat. Elliptical holes are common at the DC because the active area is tilted and some lithography schemes print the DC as the crossing of two line patterns. An ellipse 34 × 28 nm aligned with the active area places more of the floor on the node and less on the word-line caps than a circle of the same area. The model in this book uses circles. The same geometry routines handle ellipses with a change of coordinates.

5.6 Monitoring

The shrink is monitored by CD-SEM after the carbon open (on a sample of holes per die) and after the full etch on dedicated metrology. Because the bottom CD at the silicon surface cannot be seen by top-down SEM once the hole is deep, it is inferred. The inference uses the top CD and the buffer taper, calibrated by cross-section (Chapter 13).

Chapter 6 — The Buffer Open

6.1 The task

The buffer is 14 nm of SiN on 6 nm of SiO₂. The buffer open must clear both from every DC in the array without thinning the buffer elsewhere, where it is protected by the carbon mask. It must also put as little extra recess as possible into the floor materials before the recess step chooses its own selectivity. A hydrofluorocarbon (CF₄/CHF₃/O₂/Ar) step etches SiN and SiO₂ at similar rates. In the model both rates are 1.2 nm/s ion-driven plus 0.15 nm/s neutral-driven in the open field.

6.3 Overetch

The buffer overetch must cover:

  • the clear-time spread from CD (about 1.5 s over the CD range);
  • buffer thickness non-uniformity (±1 nm, about ±1 s);
  • topography of the incoming surface. Cap CMP can leave the nitride caps 2–3 nm proud of or below the silicon, and the buffer is correspondingly thinner or thicker over them.

A 15–20 % overetch (3–4 s) is typical. Because the buffer chemistry etches oxide and nitride at similar rates, and silicon several times slower, this overetch already recesses the STI and cap by about 2–3 nm before the recess step begins. It also removes the native oxide on the node and starts a fluorocarbon film on it. The recess budget in Chapter 8 includes this pre-recess.

6.4 Endpoint

The DC open area is large for a contact layer. One 32 nm hole per 3468 nm² cell pair is 23 % of the array at the buffer top and 15 % at the silicon surface. The array occupies about half of the die. Optical emission therefore shows usable transitions:

  • the CN band at 387 nm falls when the SiN layer clears into the oxide layer;
  • CO (and SiF) changes when the oxide clears onto the mixed floor.

The second transition is weak, because the floor is itself partly oxide (STI, liner) and partly nitride (caps). It is ambiguous for the same reason. The usual practice is to endpoint the SiN-to-oxide transition and time the rest of the buffer. Interferometric or reflectance endpoint on the array is also used.

6.5 Stopping on the floor

The buffer's own oxide layer is a convenient pause. A short, oxide-selective step can clear the last few nanometres of oxide while barely touching nitride or silicon, so the floor arrives at the recess step flat and clean. If the recess step uses recipe A (silicon-selective), this is the natural sequence. If it uses recipe B (co-recess), the transition matters less, because B etches all floor materials together.

6.6 Charging

The hole is lined with dielectric (carbon, SiN, SiO₂) down to a floor that is partly silicon. Electron shading charges the walls negatively and the floor positively. On a silicon floor connected to the substrate, the charge leaks away and the effect is small. On floor patches of oxide and nitride it does not, and slow ions can be deflected toward the walls or into the narrow liner slit. This is one mechanism by which the liner pit of Chapter 8 can run ahead of the model.

Chapter 7 — The Mixed-Floor Recess

7.1 The model

When the buffer clears, the floor of the hole holds four materials: node silicon, STI oxide, word-line cap nitride and gate-oxide liner. Each recesses at its own rate. The model in sim/contact_mc.py writes, for each material m,

ddₘ/dt = R_ion,m · G_ion(AR_h) · G_ion(AR_p,m) + R_n,m · G_n(AR_h) · G_n(AR_p,m)

where AR_h = (70 + 20 + d_min)/D is the aspect ratio of the hole down to the shallowest part of the floor, and AR_p,m = (dₘ − d_min)/wₘ is the aspect ratio of the pit that material m has dug below it. The pit widths are 16 nm (Si node), 8 nm (STI patch), 5 nm (cap lune) and 3.5 nm (liner). Multiplying the two transport fractions is an approximation. It treats the pit as a small hole at the floor of a larger one. It captures the main effect: a material that runs ahead slows down.

7.6 What the model omits

The model has no lateral etching, so the pit walls do not move. It has no faceting at the pit corners, no loading by the floor materials on the plasma, and no change of the fluorocarbon film with depth. Each of these shifts the numbers. None of them changes the qualitative result that the floor shape follows the selectivity, and that the narrowest features slow first.

Chapter 8 — The Word-Line Cap and the Gate-Oxide Liner

8.1 What lies beneath

On either side of the node, the DC hole overhangs a buried word line. In the reference process the hole's edge, 13 nm from the node centre, lies 1.5 nm beyond the inner edge of the word-line metal at |x| = 11.5 nm. That metal edge is the 8 nm node half-length plus the 3.5 nm gate oxide. A 6 nm overlay error along the bit line puts the hole edge over half of the 11 nm metal slot on one side. Between the bottom of the DC and the top of the word-line metal there is only the cap nitride, 60 nm in the incoming stack. Along the trench wall there is also the gate-oxide liner.

Whatever the DC etch removes from the cap and liner is margin taken from the separation between the n⁺ poly plug (at bit-line potential) and the word line (switching between about −0.3 V and the boosted level V_PP ≈ 3 V).

8.2 Computed cap and liner attack

With recipe A, the cap and liner lose less than 2 nm at the 25 nm silicon target, and the margin is essentially the incoming 60 nm. With recipe B, the cap loses 26 nm and the liner 28 nm by the time the silicon reaches 25 nm. That leaves 34 nm of cap over the metal. With recipe C run for 60 s, the cap loses 25 nm, but the liner pit runs 10 nm deeper, to 35 nm, leaving 25 nm between its tip and the metal.

8.3 Why the liner runs ahead

The liner is oxide, and recipe C etches oxide 1.7 times faster than nitride. It is also only 3.5 nm wide, so its pit enters the high-aspect-ratio regime almost at once. At a pit depth of 10 nm relative to the node, its own AR is already 2.9. These two effects oppose each other. Faster chemistry drives the liner down, and narrow-pit transport slows it. In the model the chemistry wins: the liner gains about 10 nm on the cap in 60 s. In a real process, the fluorocarbon film that deposits in a 3.5 nm slit is uncertain. Slit charging may also deflect ions into it or away from it. The real liner pit may run faster or slower than the model, and it should be measured by cross-section.

Recipe B shows the same effect more weakly. Its liner runs 1 nm ahead of the cap, because B's oxide and nitride rates are close.

8.4 What the margin protects

  • Dielectric integrity. With 3.3 V across a 34 nm nitride cap, the mean field is 1 MV/cm. At the tip of a poly-filled liner slit it is several times higher. Time-dependent breakdown of the cap between bit line and word line is a reliability failure that grows with every nanometre lost.
  • Word-line-to-bit-line leakage. Even below breakdown, trap-assisted conduction through a thin nitride adds leakage on the bit line. That leakage depends on which word line is active, and it is hard to tell apart from cell leakage.
  • Gate-induced drain leakage at the bit-line node. n⁺ poly reaching down the liner toward the word-line top brings the bit-line potential closer to the gate edge. This raises the band-to-band tunnelling field in the overlap region of the bit-line-side junction.
  • Capacitance. The added coupling is small. A few hundred nm² facing the word line through 30 nm of nitride is a fraction of an attofarad per contact. Even several hundred contacts per bit line add well under 1 % of the bit-line capacitance. Capacitance is not the reason to protect the cap.

8.6 Overlay along the bit line

All of the above assumes the hole edge lies over the metal only near its inner edge. An overlay error along the bit line moves the hole over more of the metal on one side. The vertical margin is then the only protection over a larger area. Cap loss and DC-to-word-line overlay should therefore be budgeted together: a deep cap recess is tolerable only if overlay keeps the hole's edge near the trench wall.

Part III — Contact Formation and Integration

Chapter 9 — Profile, Taper and Bottom CD

9.1 The diameter that matters

The contact touches the silicon at the bottom of the hole, not at the top. In the reference process, the 32 nm hole at the buffer top narrows to 26 nm at the silicon surface. It narrows because the buffer open is run with enough polymer to give a sidewall angle of about 81.5°. For a straight taper,

D_b = D_t − 2 H_buf cot θ,

so 20 nm of buffer at 81.5° removes 6.0 nm from the diameter. The taper is deliberate. It is a second shrink, done in the buffer, and it lets the carbon hole stay a little larger and easier to open.

9.2 Sensitivity to angle

The bottom diameter's sensitivity to sidewall angle is

∂D_b/∂θ = 2 H_buf / sin² θ,

which is 0.71 nm per degree for a 20 nm buffer at 81.5°. A 1° (1σ) scatter of the angle therefore adds about 0.7 nm (1σ) to the bottom CD. That is comparable to the 1 nm top-CD scatter. The variation model reproduces this: σ_θ = 1° alone gives σD_b = 0.72 nm (Chapter 14). The angle is set by the polymer balance in the buffer step, so it drifts with chamber condition and wafer temperature. A centre-to-edge temperature difference of a few degrees can change it by a fraction of a degree.

9.3 Profile defects

  • Bowing. Ions scattered off the carbon facets strike the buffer wall below the mask and widen it. In a 20 nm buffer the bow is small, but it widens the hole where the bit-line poly will later sit. That leaves more poly for the bit-line etch to clear (Chapter 12).
  • Necking. Excess polymer at the carbon–buffer interface narrows the hole there and shadows the floor. The bottom CD then drops more than the angle formula predicts.
  • Striations. Roughness carried down from the mask makes the bottom outline ragged. The node area changes little, because the hole overhangs the node, but the clearance to the neighbour (Chapter 10) is set by the worst point of the outline, not the mean.
  • Twisting and tilt. At an aspect ratio of 3–4, a hole is not deep enough to twist. A tilt of the whole hole from an off-normal ion flux (at the wafer edge, from a worn edge ring) shifts the bottom relative to the top. It acts as an additional overlay error at the floor.

9.4 Profile below the silicon surface

Below the silicon surface, the shape depends on the recess recipe (Chapter 7):

  • with recipe A, the pit walls are the existing interfaces between node silicon and STI oxide or gate oxide. They are as steep as the STI and word-line trench walls, typically 87–89°, and the node widens slightly with depth. A 25 nm pit in a node whose walls slope at 88° gains about 1.7 nm in width at its floor. Polymer or oxide fences at the pit corners can remove more than this from the effective area;
  • with recipe B, the hole keeps tapering through the floor. At 86° over 25 nm it loses another 3.5 nm of diameter, so a 26 nm hole reaches the bottom of the recess at about 22.5 nm. The contact is then the intersection of that smaller disk with the node (260 nm² at zero overlay, Chapter 10);
  • with recipe C, the node stands as a mesa. Its sidewalls are exposed down to the depth of the surrounding recess, and the poly wraps them.

9.5 Measuring it

Top CD is measured by CD-SEM. The bottom CD of a 26 nm hole 90 nm deep (under the mask) or 20 nm deep (after strip) can be seen with high-voltage SEM after strip, or by scatterometry on arrays of contacts. Profile and recess shape need cross-sections (TEM, STEM-EDS) on a sample basis. Cross-sections taken along the bit line show the cap lunes. Those taken along the word line show the STI recess and the neighbour.

10.1 What the contact touches

The contact area is the part of the hole bottom that lands on the node silicon. With the node a 16 nm × 17.1 nm parallelogram (274 nm²), the area depends on the hole diameter D at the silicon surface and on the overlay (oₓ, oᵧ) between the hole and the active area. The model computes it by integrating over the disk. It also reports the node edge length inside the hole (relevant when the node stands as a mesa) and the clearance from the hole edge to the nearest neighbouring active-area strip.

10.3 The price of a large hole

A larger hole buys area tolerance by spending clearance. At zero overlay the hole edge is 13.0 nm from the nearest neighbouring strip for D = 22, 11.0 nm for 26, and 9.0 nm for 30. Overlay along the word line (y) subtracts almost one-for-one: 0.93 nm of clearance per nanometre of overlay, because the strips are tilted. A 30 nm hole touches the neighbour at 10 nm of y overlay. A 26 nm hole would touch at about 12 nm. Overlay along the bit line (x) moves the hole toward the word-line caps instead, and costs clearance only slowly.

The DC diameter is therefore a trade between resistance (favours large) and neighbour isolation (favours small). The trade is resolved by the overlay distribution (Chapter 14).

10.4 From area to resistance

The resistance of the DC is modelled as

R_DC = ρ_c / A + ρ_poly L / (πD²/4),

with ρ_c = 1.5 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·cm² (1.5 × 10⁶ Ω·nm²), ρ_poly = 1 mΩ·cm and a plug length L = 45 nm. At A = 272 nm² the interface term is 5.5 kΩ and the plug about 0.85 kΩ, for 6.35 kΩ in all. The interface dominates. A 10 kΩ limit is reached when the area falls below 164 nm².

ρ_c is not a constant of nature. For a tunnelling contact it depends exponentially on the doping at the interface, roughly as exp(C φ_B / √N), with C ∝ √(ε_s m*)/ħ. It also rises steeply with any interfacial oxide. Two etch choices feed it directly:

  • Recess depth against the doping profile. The bit-line node is doped by implant and by phosphorus out-diffused from the poly plug. A deeper recess lands the interface where the implanted doping is lower, unless the out-diffusion anneal makes up the difference. A recess that is 5 nm too deep can raise ρ_c more than it gains in any other way.
  • Surface condition. Damage, halogen and carbon residues, and re-grown native oxide all raise ρ_c (Chapter 11).

10.5 Sidewall contact on a mesa

When the node stands as a mesa (recipe C, or a short C step before A), its sidewalls add contact area. At D = 26 nm and zero overlay, 63 nm of node edge lies inside the hole. A mesa 5 nm tall then adds up to 315 nm² of sidewall, more than the top. The sidewall is a poorer contact. It is plasma-exposed, it lies deeper in the doping profile, and on two sides it faces the word-line gate oxide. If only half of it counts, the effective area becomes about 430 nm² and R_DC falls to about 4.3 kΩ. The cost is in Chapter 8: the dielectric-first step that makes the mesa also recesses the cap and drives the liner pit down. A shallow mesa, a few nanometres tall, captures much of the gain at a small cost in margin.

10.6 Elliptical and self-aligned contacts

A hole elongated along the active-area direction (Chapter 5) keeps more of its area on the node under overlay along the active area. It also keeps the edge farther from the neighbours across it. Self-aligned schemes go further. The DC is patterned as a line crossing the active areas, and the cap and STI act as the mask in one direction. Overlay in that direction then drops out of the area budget, at the price of requiring high selectivity of the floor etch to those materials. In effect this is recipe A by construction (Chapter 16).

Chapter 11 — Damage, Residues and the Pre-Fill Clean

11.1 What the plasma leaves behind

The floor of the DC is the most electrically sensitive surface the etch touches. Four things are left on it or in it:

  1. Lattice damage. Ions of 50–300 eV penetrate 1–3 nm into silicon, displacing atoms and leaving an amorphised or defective layer. Br⁺ and Cl⁺ at the low bias of recipe A penetrate least. Ar⁺ and CFₓ⁺ at the higher bias of B and C penetrate most.
  2. Implanted and adsorbed species. Bromine and chlorine (recipe A), fluorine and carbon (B, C, the buffer open), and hydrogen from HBr or CHF₃ diffuse a few nanometres into the silicon. Hydrogen can go deeper and passivate dopants.
  3. Surface films. A SiOₓBrᵧ or SiOₓClᵧ layer forms from the oxygen in recipe A, and a fluorocarbon film from B and C. Both are 1–3 nm thick.
  4. Oxide. The ash that removes the carbon mask oxidises the exposed silicon if it contains oxygen, and air exposure grows native oxide afterwards.

Each raises contact resistance. The damage also adds generation centres in the bit-line junction, which increase its leakage.

11.2 Why bit-line-node damage matters less, and more

Retention is set by leakage at the storage-node junction, not the bit-line node. Damage at the DC therefore does not directly shorten retention. It matters in other ways. Bit-line junction leakage loads every bit line with many cells. It shifts the precharge level during long refresh intervals and reduces sense margin. Damage at the gate edge near the bit-line side raises GIDL there. Contact resistance from residual damage drifts with later thermal steps, which makes it hard to screen.

11.3 The strip

The carbon mask is removed by an O₂ or N₂/H₂ plasma, downstream or with low bias. O₂ is faster and cleans fluorocarbon residues well, but it oxidises the silicon floor by 1–2 nm. N₂/H₂ oxidises less, but it removes fluorocarbon films more slowly and can leave hydrogen in the silicon. Many flows strip with N₂/H₂ and rely on the wet or dry clean to remove what is left.

11.4 Damage removal

A light etch removes the damaged layer after the main recess. It is a low-energy, nearly isotropic silicon removal of 1–3 nm, either by downstream CF₄/O₂ or NF₃/O₂, or by a final low-bias step in the same chamber. It is effective because the damaged layer is thin, and costly because it also widens the pit and recesses the dielectrics slightly. The recess target should include it.

11.5 The pre-fill clean

Before the poly fill, the floor must be free of oxide and residue. Two families are used:

  • Wet. Dilute HF (100:1 to 500:1) removes the native and ash oxide and loosens halogen and fluorocarbon films. It also etches the STI and the gate-oxide liner. At 1–2 nm of oxide removal, the liner pit deepens by about as much (Chapter 8). An SC1 step before the HF helps remove particles and organic residue, but it re-oxidises the surface.
  • Dry. A remote NF₃/NH₃ plasma forms ammonium fluorosilicate on oxide surfaces. A bake at about 100–180 °C sublimes it. The reaction self-limits on silicon and etches oxide more uniformly in narrow features than wet HF, since it does not depend on liquid transport into a 3.5 nm slit. It is often integrated on the same platform as the poly deposition, so the clean surface never sees air.

11.6 Queue time

Native oxide regrows on clean silicon at a few tenths of a nanometre per hour in clean-room air. A tunnelling contact is very sensitive to interfacial oxide: half a nanometre can raise ρ_c several-fold. A queue-time limit between clean and fill, typically a few hours, is enforced for the DC. An integrated pre-clean in the fill tool removes the limit and is the preferred practice at advanced nodes.

Chapter 12 — Polysilicon Fill and the Hand-off to the Bit-Line Stack

12.1 The fill

The DC is filled with in-situ phosphorus-doped silicon by LPCVD. It is usually deposited amorphous at 500–550 °C and crystallised by later anneals. The deposition is conformal. It closes the 26–32 nm hole from the walls inward and leaves a seam on the hole axis. In a recipe-A pit (16 nm wide, 25 nm deep, AR 1.6) the pit fills first and the seam starts above it. If the pit is wider at the top than at the bottom, which is the normal case, the seam closes cleanly. If its walls overhang, because of corner fences or a re-entrant STI profile, a void can close inside the pit, where it removes contact area directly.

The gate-oxide liner slit, if a dielectric-first or wet step has opened it, is 3.5 nm wide. Poly enters it and pinches off, leaving a thin n⁺ fin that points down toward the word-line metal (Chapter 8).

12.2 Doping and the bit-line junction

The plug is doped at about 10²⁰–10²¹ cm⁻³. During later anneals, phosphorus diffuses from the plug into the node and joins the implanted bit-line junction. A few nanometres of out-diffusion make the contact ohmic even if the recess has landed below the implant peak. Excess diffusion moves the junction toward the word-line gate edge and raises GIDL. The recess depth and the thermal budget are therefore set together.

12.3 Planarisation

The fill overburden is removed by poly CMP or etchback, stopping on the buffer nitride. The plug top is left flush or slightly recessed. The plug height (about 45 nm from the recess floor to the buffer top) enters the plug resistance. Its variation is set by CMP dishing in the dense DC array.

12.4 What the bit-line etch inherits

The bit-line stack (barrier, tungsten, nitride cap) is deposited on the buffer and plug, and etched into lines 20 nm wide at the bottom (Book 29). Where a line crosses a DC, the poly beside it must be removed down to below the level where the storage-node contact will land. Otherwise the storage-node contact touches the poly and shorts bit line to storage node.

The gap beside the line is set by the DC, not by the line. At the buffer top it is (32 − 20)/2 = 6 nm per side at perfect overlay. At the silicon surface the DC has tapered to 26 nm, and the gap is (26 − 20)/2 = 3 nm. In a recipe-B or recipe-C floor, the poly fills a recess below that as well. The bit-line etch must therefore clear poly from a gap that narrows with depth. The taper that helped the contact (Chapter 9) works against the bit-line etch. Book 29 (Chapter 8) shows that a 4 nm gap does not clear in any practical overetch.

Three resolutions are used:

  • Keep the gap wider at depth. Use a less tapered DC, accepting a larger top CD.
  • Make the plug narrower than the hole. A nitride liner in the DC before the fill turns the hole into a plug with insulating sides. The liner protects the cap and STI and moves the bit-line etch's gap outward.
  • Seal what remains. A nitride gap-fill after the bit-line etch insulates any poly left deep in the gap. This works if the residue lies below the storage-node contact's landing level.

12.5 The pockets beside the plug

A recipe-B or recipe-C floor recesses the STI and caps beside the node by 25–40 nm. After the bit-line etch removes the poly there, these pockets are open cavities next to the bit line. The bit-line spacer and gap-fill must fill them. The storage-node contact etch must then cut through that fill to reach the storage node. Deep pockets therefore make the storage-node contact etch longer and the spacer harder to fill. This cost of a non-selective DC recess appears two modules later.

Part IV — Control, Variation and Outlook

Chapter 13 — Reactor, Recipe, Endpoint and Metrology

13.1 One chamber or two

The DC etch spans two kinds of chemistry. The mask open, buffer open and any fluorocarbon floor step (B or C) belong in a dielectric etch chamber, usually a capacitively coupled reactor with a high-frequency source and a low-frequency bias. The silicon-selective recess (A) belongs in a conductor etch chamber, an inductively coupled reactor with an independent bias, often pulsed. Three arrangements are common:

  • two chambers: mask and buffer in a dielectric chamber, recess in a conductor chamber on the same platform, with a vacuum transfer between them;
  • one dielectric chamber: all steps fluorocarbon-based, with the floor recess done by recipe B and a short HBr-free silicon step. This is simpler, but it pays in cap and liner margin;
  • one conductor chamber: mask and buffer opened in an ICP with fluorocarbon gases, then HBr/Cl₂ for the recess. This is possible because the buffer is thin, but polymer memory effects between steps must be managed by in-situ cleans.

13.3 Endpoint

The SiON, carbon and buffer-nitride steps endpoint on optical emission. The DC open area is large (15–23 % of the array) and the transitions are clean: SiON to carbon (CO rises), carbon to SiN (CO falls, CN rises), SiN to oxide (CN falls). The oxide-to-floor transition is ambiguous because the floor is mixed (Chapter 6), so the last nanometres of buffer are timed.

The recess has no endpoint. Nothing in the plasma changes when the silicon passes 25 nm. It is run on time, with two corrections:

  • feed-forward from CD. In recipe A the recess at a fixed time moves by 0.55 nm per nanometre of bottom CD (Chapter 7). At a rate of about 0.5 nm/s, this is about 1.1 s of time per nanometre of CD. A lot-average CD measured after the carbon open can be fed forward to the recess time;
  • feedback from recess metrology, by scatterometry on array targets or by sampled cross-section. This corrects slow drifts of rate with chamber condition.

13.4 Chamber condition

HBr/Cl₂/O₂ deposits SiOₓBrᵧ on the chamber walls. Fluorocarbon steps deposit polymer. When both run in one chamber, each step's walls become the next step's initial condition. A waferless clean (SF₆/O₂ or NF₃) between wafers, followed by a short seasoning, keeps the first-wafer effect small. The recess rate is especially sensitive to wall state, because recipe A's neutral term depends on the recombination of Br and Cl on the walls.

13.6 What to control

The variation budget (Chapter 14) shows that overlay dominates the DC's failure rate and that CD matters less. The etch controls the bottom CD through the buffer taper, and the recess depth through time. Its main levers on yield are to keep the hole near the node-limited regime, so that area is insensitive, and to keep the cap and liner margin intact, so that the word line is safe.

Chapter 14 — The Variation Budget

14.1 The model

Four sources of variation are sampled per contact in a Monte Carlo of 200 000 contacts:

  • top CD at the buffer top, nominal 32 nm, standard deviation σ_CD;
  • buffer sidewall angle, nominal 81.5°, standard deviation σ_θ, which turns into bottom-CD scatter through D_b = D_t − 2H_buf cot θ;
  • overlay of the DC to the active area, independently in x and y, each with standard deviation σ_ov;
  • node scale: all lateral dimensions of the cell and the hole multiplied by 1, 0.88 or 0.76 (F = 17, 15, 13 nm), with the variation kept as stated.

For each contact the model computes the area on the node, the resistance R_DC = ρ_c/A + plug, and the clearance from the hole edge to the nearest neighbouring active-area strip. A contact fails high if R_DC > 10 kΩ. It touches a neighbour if the clearance is below zero. In the scaled rows, ρ_c is multiplied by the stated factor to represent interface improvements.

14.3 Reading the table

  • CD and angle barely matter at the reference node. A 1 nm (1σ) top-CD scatter gives an area scatter of only 2 nm². A 1° angle scatter gives 0.72 nm of bottom-CD scatter but only 1.6 nm² of area scatter. The 26 nm hole is node-limited (Chapter 10). Its area is set by the node, not by the hole.
  • Overlay dominates. A 2 nm (1σ per axis) overlay alone gives an area scatter of 7.5 nm² and lowers the mean area by 7 nm². Combined with modest CD and angle scatter (1 nm, 0.5°), it yields about 10 ppm of high-resistance contacts and no touches in 200 000.
  • Poor control is catastrophic. At 1.5 nm, 1° and 3 nm, the high-resistance rate is 0.5 % and touches appear at about 400 ppm.
  • Shrinking the node without shrinking overlay fails. At F = 15 nm with the same variation and the same interface, the area falls to 204 nm². The median resistance rises to 8.2 kΩ, and 0.8 % of contacts exceed 10 kΩ. A 40 % lower ρ_c restores the median to 5.3 kΩ and the tail to about 5 ppm. At F = 13 nm, even a halved ρ_c leaves 275 ppm high and 25 ppm touching. Only when CD, angle and overlay variation scale down with the node (×0.7) do both failure rates vanish from the sample.

14.4 How many failures can be tolerated

A 16 Gb die has 8 × 10⁹ bit-line contacts, each serving two cells. Redundancy can repair failing bits at a rate of perhaps 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁵, depending on clustering. A DC failure rate of 10 ppm (10⁻⁵) consumes all of it. The rows that pass in this table are therefore only just acceptable. The model's resolution is one failing contact in 200 000, or 5 ppm, so rates of 5–25 ppm rest on one to five events. A production budget needs margin well below the table's resolution. It must be set by extrapolating the tail (for example from the Gaussian margin-to-failure in units of σ), not by counting.

14.5 Allocating the budget

The area is approximately a function of the radial overlay o = √(oₓ² + oᵧ²) once the hole is node-limited. It stays near the node area until o reaches the "free" overlay o_f, where the hole edge reaches the node corner, and falls beyond it. For the 26 nm hole o_f is about 3–4 nm. If o has Rayleigh distribution with scale σ_ov, the probability of exceeding a radial error o_c is exp(−o_c²/2σ_ov²). With o_c ≈ 10 nm (where A reaches 164 nm² along either axis) and σ_ov = 2 nm, this is exp(−12.5) ≈ 4 × 10⁻⁶, consistent with no failures in the overlay-only row. Adding CD and angle scatter moves the failure boundary inward and brings the rate up to the 10 ppm of the combined row. Because the exponent scales as 1/σ_ov², each 10 % reduction in σ_ov multiplies it by 1.23, which at this margin cuts the failure rate about twentyfold.

The etch owns the bottom CD (through the buffer taper) and the recess. Lithography owns the top CD and overlay. The budget is best allocated by keeping the hole node-limited, so that CD is cheap, and spending the margin on overlay.

14.6 What is not in the model

The model leaves out recess-depth variation, which matters through ρ_c via the doping profile (Chapter 10). It also leaves out the shape of the hole outline (striations, ellipticity) and within-node doping variation. Each adds scatter to the resistance. The model treats every contact independently. Real overlay errors are correlated over a field, so failures cluster, and clustering changes what redundancy can repair.

15.2 The paired-bit signature

Two cells share every DC. They lie on the same bit line and on the two adjacent word lines that cross their active area. A DC that is open or highly resistive therefore fails both cells together. The bitmap shows a pair of vertically adjacent failing bits in one column. This paired-bit failure is the strongest diagnostic link from test back to the DC module. A random single-bit failure points elsewhere (capacitor, storage-node contact, storage junction). A paired-bit failure points to the DC or the bit-line node.

Pair failures that cluster at the wafer edge suggest tilt or bottom-CD loss from the edge ring. Clustering along one direction of a lithography field suggests overlay. Pair failures that depend on test temperature and timing suggest high resistance rather than an open.

15.3 Word-line-dependent failures

A bit line that fails only when a particular word line is selected is leaking to or shorted to that word line. Where the DC is the cause, the failing word line is one of the two adjacent to a DC on that bit line. If cap loss or a liner pit is responsible, the failure is often voltage-dependent and may grow with stress. That makes it a reliability failure as well as a yield failure.

15.4 Reliability

  • Cap dielectric breakdown. Between the DC plug and the word line, the cap nitride sees the word-line swing for the life of the part. Time-dependent dielectric breakdown accelerates with field. A cap thinned from 60 to 25 nm more than doubles the mean field. At a poly-filled liner fin, the field is concentrated further. Burn-in screens the weakest contacts. The margin of Chapter 8 must cover the rest.
  • Contact resistance drift. Halogen residues and interfacial oxide can change during later anneals and under current. A contact near the resistance limit at test may drift beyond it. Excursions in clean quality are therefore reliability issues as well as yield issues.
  • Row-hammer and disturbance. A plug close to the word line (deep cap loss, liner fin) increases coupling between word-line activity and the bit-line node. Its effect is small next to the main row-hammer mechanisms, but it adds to the leakage of the bit-line-side junction under repeated activation.

15.5 Test structures

The DC module is qualified with:

  • Kelvin DC chains of thousands of contacts, alternating poly plugs and active-area segments, to measure R_DC and its tail;
  • combs of DCs against word lines, to measure BL–WL leakage and breakdown;
  • DC-to-neighbour structures in which the neighbouring storage nodes are wired separately, to measure DC–SN isolation;
  • array test with bitmap analysis classified by failure pattern (single bit, paired bit, column, row).

15.6 Connecting back to the etch

Each signature has a measurable counterpart in the etch module. Paired-bit failures correlate with bottom CD, overlay and voltage-contrast opens. BL–WL failures correlate with cap remaining and liner pit depth in cross-section. DC–SN failures correlate with y overlay and with poly residue in the bit-line etch. Junction leakage correlates with recess recipe and clean. Keeping these correlations up to date is the main job of yield engineering for the DC.

Chapter 16 — The Contact in Future DRAM

16.1 Planar 6F² scaling

Chapter 14 showed what happens when the reference cell shrinks from F = 17 to 13 nm with overlay unchanged. The contact area falls with F², and the resistance tail and neighbour touches appear together. Three changes keep the planar DC alive:

  • Lower ρ_c. Higher active doping at the interface, oxide-free interfaces from integrated dry cleans, and epitaxial rather than polycrystalline plugs. A selectively grown epitaxial silicon or SiP plug removes the grain boundaries and the interfacial oxide of the poly–Si interface. It can halve ρ_c or better.
  • Self-aligned DCs. The DC is printed as a line crossing many active areas, and the word-line caps and STI act as the mask in one direction. Overlay in that direction drops out of the area budget. The floor etch must then be strongly silicon-selective (recipe A by construction), and the cap must be thick enough to serve as a mask.
  • Atomic-layer recess. Cyclic Cl₂ adsorption and low-energy Ar⁺ removal recesses silicon at a fixed amount per cycle, with little damage and almost no aspect-ratio dependence. Recess control of ±1 nm becomes possible. The cost is throughput.

16.2 Metal contacts

Replacing the poly plug with a metal (TiSiₓ/TiN/W or a ruthenium or molybdenum plug on a silicide) lowers the plug term and allows a silicide interface. The DC etch then has to leave a surface fit for silicidation. That requires low damage and no oxide, and the poly out-diffusion that previously compensated for a deep recess is gone.

16.3 4F² vertical-channel DRAM

In a 4F² cell with a vertical-channel transistor (VCT), the transistor stands as a silicon pillar. The bit line is buried below the pillars, or bonded onto their bottom after wafer flipping, and the storage node sits on top. The bit-line contact becomes the bottom junction of the pillar. It is formed by doping or silicidation at the pillar base, not by an etched hole. Its etch challenges move to the pillar etch and the buried bit-line trench. Isolating a metal bit line from the pillar's gate a few nanometres away is a related problem. The trade-off between contact area and isolation moves with it.

16.4 3D DRAM

In stacked 3D DRAM, horizontal transistors are stacked in tens of layers and the bit line runs vertically through the stack. Each layer's transistor must contact the vertical bit line, so the bit-line contact becomes a high-aspect-ratio vertical hole or slot, much like a 3D NAND channel hole. Its sidewall must make contact to a silicon layer in every tier. The transport problems of Chapter 4 return at aspect ratios of 30–60. The mixed-floor problem of Chapter 7 becomes a mixed-sidewall problem: silicon tiers between dielectric tiers, each to be recessed, cleaned and contacted uniformly from top to bottom.

16.5 What carries over

Whatever the cell architecture, the bit-line contact has the same four jobs: conduct, stay clear of the word line, stay clear of the neighbour, and leave the junction healthy. The quantitative tools in this book carry over directly: transport fractions in a hole, rate laws for a mixed floor, area-against-overlay geometry, and a variation budget counted in ppm. So does the lesson of the reference process: the floor's shape follows the selectivity, the area follows the overlay, and the margin is whatever the etch did not remove.

Appendix A — The Monte Carlo Method

A.1 Geometry

The contact hole is a cylinder of radius 1 (all lengths in units of the hole radius) and depth H = 2 AR, where AR is the depth divided by the diameter, measured from the top of the remaining mask. z increases downward from the opening at z = 0 to the floor at z = H.

A.2 Neutrals

Each neutral starts at a uniformly random point on the opening disk with a direction drawn from the cosine (Lambertian) distribution about +z. It travels in a straight line to the first of: the wall (solving |(x, y) + t(dₓ, dᵧ)| = 1 for the positive root), the floor, or the opening. At the wall it is lost with probability s. Otherwise it is re-emitted from the wall point with a new cosine direction about the inward normal. The floor fraction is the number that reach z = H divided by the number launched. 200 000 particles are used per point.

A.3 Ions

Each ion starts at a uniformly random point on the opening with independent Gaussian tilts of width σ in x and y. Because the cylinder is convex, a straight trajectory that starts and ends inside it stays inside it. An ion therefore reaches the floor if and only if its landing point (x + H tan θₓ, y + H tan θᵧ) lies inside the unit disk.

Appendix E — Derivations

E.1 View factor of a cylinder's floor from its opening

For two coaxial parallel disks of radii r₁, r₂ separated by H, with Rᵢ = rᵢ/H and X = 1 + (1 + R₂²)/R₁², the view factor is F₁₂ = ½[X − √(X² − 4(R₂/R₁)²)]. For equal radii R = r/H = 1/(2 AR), so X = 2 + 4 AR² and F = ½(X − √(X² − 4)). For large AR, F → 1/X ≈ 1/(4 AR²). The corresponding slot result is √(1 + AR²) − AR → 1/(2 AR). At large AR the hole therefore passes a factor of about 2 AR fewer line-of-sight neutrals than the slot.

E.2 Ion floor fraction for small σ

An ion tilted by a small angle vector θ lands displaced by δ = H|θ|. The fraction of a disk of radius r that stays inside after a shift δ is the lens overlap of two circles, ≈ 1 − 2δ/(πr) for δ ≪ r. |θ| has a Rayleigh distribution with mean σ√(π/2). Averaging, with H = 2 AR r,

G_ion ≈ 1 − (4/√(2π)) AR σ ≈ 1 − 1.60 AR σ (σ in radians).

At AR = 1 and σ = 2° (0.0349 rad), G_ion ≈ 0.944, as computed (0.9445). The slot coefficient is 0.80 (Book 29). The hole loses ions twice as fast, because both directions of tilt count.

E.3 Bottom CD from taper

A hole of top diameter D_t through a layer of thickness H with wall angle θ has bottom diameter D_b = D_t − 2H cot θ. Its sensitivity is ∂D_b/∂θ = 2H/sin²θ per radian, which is 0.71 nm per degree for H = 20 nm and θ = 81.5°.

E.4 Area at the resistance limit

With R_DC = ρ_c/A + R_plug, the limit R_max is reached at A_min = ρ_c/(R_max − R_plug) = 1.5 × 10⁶/(10 000 − 847) ≈ 164 nm².

E.5 Recess sensitivity to CD and the feed-forward gain

If the recess at fixed time changes by ∂d/∂D per nanometre of CD and the rate at the end of the step is r, the time correction that restores the target is Δt = −(∂d/∂D) ΔD/r. For recipe A, (0.55 nm/nm)/(0.49 nm/s) ≈ 1.1 s per nm.

E.6 Rayleigh tail of radial overlay

If oₓ and oᵧ are independent normal with standard deviation σ, the radial error o is Rayleigh distributed and P(o > o_c) = exp(−o_c²/2σ²). Since the exponent scales as 1/σ², a fractional reduction ε in σ multiplies it by 1/(1 − ε)².

E.7 Pit transport

A pit of width w and depth p at the floor of a hole receives the flux that reaches the hole floor, F_h. Of that, a fraction G(p/w) reaches the pit bottom. The model uses the round-hole transport fractions for both factors. This is an approximation: the flux entering the pit is not cosine-distributed, and the pits are not round. It is used for its qualitative behaviour (narrow, deep pits slow down), not for precise rates.

Appendix F — Worked Problems

F.1 A DC is 31 nm at the buffer top. The 20 nm buffer is etched at 82°. What is the bottom CD, and is the contact node-limited? Answer: 2 × 20 × cot 82° = 5.6 nm, so D_b = 25.4 nm. Between the 22 and 26 nm rows of Chapter 10 the area at zero overlay is 260–272 nm², 95–99 % of the node. The contact is nearly node-limited.

F.2 With recipe A, a lot's mean bottom CD is 24.5 nm instead of 26 nm. How should the recess time change? Answer: the recess at fixed time drops by 0.55 × 1.5 ≈ 0.8 nm. At 0.49 nm/s, add about 1.7 s.

F.3 The process uses recipe B to 25 nm of silicon recess. The incoming cap is 2 nm below the Si surface, the buffer overetch removes 3 nm, and the clean removes 2 nm of liner oxide. How much cap is left over the word-line metal under the liner pit? Answer: 60 − 2 − 3 − 27.6 − 2 ≈ 25 nm, below a 30 nm minimum. Shorten the B step, or switch to A for the last 15 nm.

F.4 A DC at 26 nm bottom CD has 6 nm of overlay along the word line. Estimate R_DC and the clearance to the neighbour. Answer: from Appendix B.6, area 235 nm², so R = 1.5 × 10⁶/235 + 847 ≈ 7.2 kΩ. The clearance is 5.4 nm.

F.5 Overlay is 1.8 nm (1σ per axis) and resistance fails above 10 nm of radial error. Estimate the failure rate. Answer: exp(−100/(2 × 3.24)) = exp(−15.4) ≈ 2 × 10⁻⁷, about 0.2 ppm.

F.6 A mesa 4 nm tall is left by a short dielectric-first step at D = 26 nm. If the sidewall counts at half the efficiency of the top, what is the effective area and R_DC? Answer: 272 + 0.5 × 63 × 4 = 398 nm²; R = 1.5 × 10⁶/398 + 847 ≈ 4.6 kΩ.

Glossary

Active area (AA) — Silicon bar between STI regions that holds the two access transistors of a cell pair.

ARDE — Aspect-ratio-dependent etching; here, slower recess in smaller holes and in narrow pits at the floor.

Aspect ratio (AR) — Depth from the top of the mask divided by hole diameter (or pit depth divided by pit width).

ACL (amorphous carbon layer) — Carbon hard mask used to transfer the DC hole.

BCAT — Buried-channel array transistor; the cell transistor whose gate is a buried word line.

Bit line (BL) — Column conductor that carries charge between the cells and the sense amplifier.

Bit-line node — Diffusion at the centre of the active area, shared by two cells and contacted by the DC.

Buffer — Thin oxide/nitride layer between the planarised surface and the bit line; opened at each DC.

Cap (word-line cap) — Silicon-nitride fill above the recessed word-line metal.

CD shrink — Reduction of the hole diameter during mask open by wall passivation and taper.

Clearance — Distance from the DC edge to the nearest neighbouring active-area strip.

Co-recess — Floor etch that removes silicon, oxide and nitride at similar rates (recipe B).

Contact resistance (R_DC) — Series resistance of the DC: interface term ρ_c/A plus the poly plug.

DC (direct contact) — The bit-line contact: a poly-filled hole connecting the bit line to the bit-line node.

Dielectric-first — Floor etch that removes oxide and nitride much faster than silicon, leaving a mesa (recipe C).

Dry clean — Remote-plasma NF₃/NH₃ oxide removal with a sublimation bake.

GIDL — Gate-induced drain leakage: band-to-band tunnelling where a gate overlaps a junction.

Kelvin chain — Test structure of many contacts measured with separate force and sense leads.

Light etch — Low-energy, near-isotropic removal of a few nanometres of damaged silicon.

Liner (gate-oxide liner) — Gate oxide on the word-line trench wall; a 3.5 nm oxide sliver in the DC floor.

Liner pit — Slit etched down the gate-oxide liner, deeper than the cap around it.

LCDU — Local CD uniformity: hole-to-hole CD variation within a small area.

Mesa — Node silicon left standing above recessed dielectrics.

Mixed floor — DC floor made of node silicon, STI oxide, cap nitride and gate-oxide liner.

Node-limited — Regime in which the hole covers the node and contact area is set by the node, not the hole.

OCD — Optical critical-dimension metrology (scatterometry).

Overlay — Placement error of the DC relative to the active area.

Paired-bit failure — Failure of the two cells that share one DC: adjacent rows, same column.

Pit — Recess of the node silicon below the surrounding dielectrics (recipe A).

Recess — Etch of the floor materials below the silicon surface after the buffer clears.

ρ_c (specific contact resistivity) — Interface resistance times area, Ω·cm².

SNC (storage-node contact) — Contact from a storage node to its capacitor; lands beside the bit line.

STI — Shallow trench isolation: oxide between active areas.

Taper — Inward slope of the hole wall; reduces bottom CD.

Transport fraction (G) — Fraction of ions or neutrals entering a feature that reach its floor.

View factor — Fraction of diffuse line-of-sight radiation (or s = 1 neutrals) leaving one surface that reaches another.

Voltage contrast — E-beam inspection that shows open or resistive contacts by their charging.

Wall loss (s) — Probability that a neutral is lost at each wall collision.

Word line (WL) — Row conductor; in BCAT DRAM a buried metal line forming the transistor gates.
